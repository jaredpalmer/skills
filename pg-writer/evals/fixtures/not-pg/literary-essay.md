The Weight of Small Rooms

My grandmother kept her sewing things in a tin that had once held Danish butter cookies, and for most of my childhood I believed, with the unshakable faith of the very young, that every such tin in the world contained cookies only as a kind of disguise. The blue lid with its painted windmill was a promise the tin had never meant to keep. Inside there were spools of thread in colors that no longer existed, a thimble dented on one side, needles pushed through a square of felt like the pins on a general's map, and a single mother-of-pearl button that belonged to nothing.

I think of that tin more often than I would like to admit. It was the first object I understood to have a secret life, a gap between what it announced and what it held, and the discovery did not disappoint me so much as rearrange me. Afterward I looked at every closed thing, every drawer and envelope and shuttered house on the long road to her farm, as though it were withholding something I was owed.

She lived in a house of small rooms. The kitchen was the size of a generous closet, and the parlor, which nobody used, was smaller still, a museum of antimacassars and a clock that ticked with the deliberation of a man counting out change. It was in these rooms that I first learned the particular quality of attention that confinement produces. When there is little space, every object is seen; when there is little to do, every hour is felt. My grandmother did not believe in boredom. She believed in mending, which is a different thing, a way of meeting time with your hands instead of your restlessness.

There is a school of thought, fashionable now, that treats smallness as a failure of ambition. Our houses have grown, our screens have grown, our appetites for experience have grown until a life that does not include a certain number of continents seems somehow undernourished. I understand the argument, and I have lived, for long stretches, according to it. Yet I have never felt the world as sharply as I did at eight years old, sitting on a braided rug in a room I could cross in four steps, sorting buttons by the light of a window that faced a field of stubbled corn.

Perhaps what the small rooms taught me was not contentment, exactly, but scale. A person can hold only so much. The mind, like the tin, has a lid; it can be filled with thread or with cookies or with a single orphaned button, but it cannot be filled with everything, and the fantasy that it can is its own kind of poverty. My grandmother knew this without ever having to say it. She chose what to keep. She kept it well.

When she died, the tin came to me. I have not opened it in years. I know what is inside, or I believe I do, and some part of me, still eight, still suspicious of lids, would rather not find out that I am wrong.
