The Boring Moat

Most founders think hard problems look impressive. They imagine rockets, or new kinds of chips, or research that ends up in Nature.

That's the wrong picture.

The hard problems that make good companies usually look dull. Payroll. Compliance. Procurement. Insurance paperwork. Nobody writes breathless profiles about the startup that reconciles invoices. And yet that startup is often the one that's still around in ten years.

Why?

The obvious answer is that these markets are big. They are. But that isn't the interesting part. The interesting part is why they stay open. They stay open because they're boring, and boredom repels founders more reliably than difficulty does.

I call this the schlep filter. A problem that is merely hard attracts smart people. A problem that is hard and boring attracts almost no one. The work is tedious. The edge cases never end. The customers are slow. So the ambitious founders go build something shinier, and the incumbents, who know nobody is coming, stop trying.

The dullness isn't a cost. It's the moat.

Think about what it takes to actually fix payroll. You have to understand tax rules in fifty states. You have to handle the employee who moved halfway through the year. You have to deal with the bank that sends files in a format from 1987. None of this is intellectually glamorous. All of it is load-bearing.

And every one of those edge cases is a wall a competitor has to climb.

Here's what most people miss. The knowledge you pick up while living in the mess doesn't just help you. It compounds. Each weird case you handle makes the next one easier to see. After a few years you understand the problem better than anyone, including the incumbents, and that understanding is very hard to copy.

The mess is the product.

AI makes this more true, not less. It's getting easier every month to build an impressive demo. Anyone can now make something that looks good in a pitch meeting. So looking good is worth less. What's worth more is the stuff that doesn't show up in demos: the thousand small encounters with reality that a real product has to survive.

Boring software has more reality in it. That's the whole trick.

So what should founders do? Look for problems that make you slightly sad to think about. Look for the spreadsheet that an entire department maintains by hand. Look for the job everyone complains about but nobody tries to fix. These are signs of a market that the ambitious have quietly agreed to ignore.

It won't feel exciting. It isn't supposed to.

The best companies often look boring from the outside and fascinating from the inside. The closer you get, the more interesting the mess becomes. And the more interesting it becomes, the less likely anyone else is to follow you in.

That's not a bug. That's the point.
