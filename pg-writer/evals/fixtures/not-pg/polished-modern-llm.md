On the Economics of Expertise in an Age of Capable Machines

There is a comfortable story circulating about artificial intelligence and skill: that the machines will level the field, that the novice equipped with a capable model will soon stand shoulder to shoulder with the veteran, and that the long apprenticeship of learning a craft is becoming an anachronism. The story has an appealing symmetry to it; it flatters the newcomer and absolves everyone of the obligation to learn difficult things. It is also, I suspect, mostly wrong.

Consider what actually happens when a seasoned engineer sits down with one of these systems. She does not simply accept what the model produces. She interrogates it, recognizes the subtle error in the third function, notices that the proposed architecture will collapse under a particular load pattern, and redirects the model toward a better approach within seconds. The model amplifies her judgment; it does not replace it. The novice, by contrast, receives the same output and has no way to evaluate it. He ships the subtle error. He adopts the fragile architecture. His gains are real, but they are bounded by his inability to distinguish good output from plausible output.

This asymmetry is the crux of the matter. The value of a capable tool is not distributed evenly across its users; it accrues disproportionately to those who can direct it. What emerges is a convex relationship between expertise and output, in which small differences in underlying knowledge translate into large differences in what a person can accomplish. The expert in a familiar domain experiences something closer to multiplication than addition.

The implications for how we think about learning are considerable. If the relationship were linear, one might reasonably conclude that deep expertise matters less than it once did, since the tool supplies a baseline of competence to everyone. But convexity inverts this conclusion. Under convexity, each additional increment of genuine understanding is worth more than the last, and the returns to mastery grow rather than shrink. The advice to skip learning to code, then, gets the economics precisely backwards.

There is a further subtlety worth drawing out. Expertise is not a single quantity but a landscape, uneven and particular. An engineer who has spent a decade in distributed systems may be a novice in computational biology. The same tool that makes her extraordinary in one domain makes her merely competent in another. This anisotropy suggests that the relevant question is not whether someone is an expert but where, and how far into the tails of that domain their knowledge extends. The rare, out-of-distribution knowledge that models have seen least often is precisely where human judgment remains most valuable.

What this means for organizations is harder to predict, though the broad contours seem clear enough. Firms will likely become more dependent on a smaller number of deeply skilled people, each directing a great deal of machine effort. The middle tier of competent generalists, whose value lay in reliably executing well-understood tasks, will face the greatest pressure. The shape of the curve matters enormously here; a steep curve implies a winner-take-most labor market, while a gentler one leaves more room for broad participation.

None of this is cause for despair, but it is cause for a certain seriousness. The machines have not made learning obsolete; they have raised its stakes. The person who invests in genuine understanding now is buying a position on the steep part of a curve that is only getting steeper, and that is a trade worth making.
