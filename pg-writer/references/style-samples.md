# Paul Graham, Move by Move

Real passages from PG essays, grouped by what they're doing rather than by essay. Every quote is verbatim; `[...]` marks a cut. The notes say what to notice. They aren't templates. If you catch yourself filling one in with new nouns, you've learned the wrong lesson.

Read the whole file before drafting. You're trying to absorb three things: how he gets from a question to an idea, how he sounds while doing it, and how plainly he stops.

---

## 1. Openings

He starts with the idea or something concrete. He doesn't announce that an essay is coming. The first sentence is usually plain, and the pull comes from what it implies.

> The most damaging thing you learned in school wasn't something you learned in any specific class. It was learning to get good grades.
> — The Lesson to Unlearn (2019)

> In the early 1970s disaster struck the Swiss watch industry. Now people call it the quartz crisis, but in fact it was a compound of three separate disasters that all happened at about the same time.
> — The Brand Age (2026)

> It struck me recently how few of the most successful people I know are mean. There are exceptions, but remarkably few.
> — Mean People Fail (2014)

> Before I had kids, I was afraid of having kids. Up to that point I felt about kids the way the young Augustine felt about living virtuously. I'd have been sad to think I'd never have children. But did I want them now? No.
> — Having Kids (2019)

> There's some debate about whether it's a good idea to "follow your passion." In fact the question is impossible to answer with a simple yes or no. Sometimes you should and sometimes you shouldn't, but the border between should and shouldn't is very complicated. The only way to give a general answer is to trace it.
> — When To Do What You Love (2024)

> I've read Villehardouin's chronicle of the Fourth Crusade at least two times, maybe three. And yet if I had to write down everything I remember from it, I doubt it would amount to much more than a page. Multiply this times several hundred, and I get an uneasy feeling when I look at my bookshelves. What use is it to read all these books if I remember so little from them?
> — How You Know (2014)

What to notice:
- The openings range from a personal fact to a historical event to a flat claim. None of them clears its throat.
- "The Lesson to Unlearn" leads with a strong claim, but it's a *specific* one (grades, not "school is broken"), and the reader immediately wants to know why.
- "When To Do What You Love" opens by saying the question is hard and promising to trace it. That's about as close to stating a plan as he gets, and even there he's really stating a finding: yes/no is the wrong shape of answer.
- The Villehardouin opening arrives at its question through arithmetic ("Multiply this times several hundred"). The concrete detail is what makes the question feel real.

## 2. A whole short essay, compressed

"Writes and Write-Nots" (2024) is about 500 words. Here's the spine of it:

> I'm usually reluctant to make predictions about technology, but I feel fairly confident about this one: in a couple decades there won't be many people who can write.
>
> One of the strangest things you learn if you're a writer is how many people have trouble writing. Doctors know how many people have a mole they're worried about; people who are good at setting up computers know how many people aren't; writers know how many people need help writing.
>
> The reason so many people have trouble writing is that it's fundamentally difficult. To write well you have to think clearly, and thinking clearly is hard.
>
> [...]
>
> Not anymore. AI has blown this world open. Almost all pressure to write has dissipated. You can have AI do it for you, both in school and at work.
>
> [...]
>
> Is that so bad? Isn't it common for skills to disappear when technology makes them obsolete? There aren't many blacksmiths left, and it doesn't seem to be a problem.
>
> Yes, it's bad. The reason is something I mentioned earlier: writing is thinking. In fact there's a kind of thinking that can only be done by writing. [...]
>
> This situation is not unprecedented. In preindustrial times most people's jobs made them strong. Now if you want to be strong, you work out. So there are still strong people, but only those who choose to be.
>
> It will be the same with writing. There will still be smart people, but only those who choose to be.

The arc goes prediction, then why it's plausible, then what changed, then the obvious objection in the reader's own words, then the answer, then a historical parallel that makes the conclusion feel inevitable. Each paragraph does one job. The objection gets a real hearing ("There aren't many blacksmiths left"). The ending is the parallel carried one step, and it's funny without trying to be.

## 3. Following the thread somewhere unexpected

This is the move that most separates his essays from competent imitations. He lets the argument surprise him, and he says so.

> If the question of how to write the best possible essay reduces to the question of how to make great discoveries, then I started with the wrong question. Perhaps what this exercise shows is that we shouldn't waste our time writing essays but instead focus on making discoveries in some specific domain. But I'm interested in essays and what can be done with them, so I want to see if there's some other question I could have asked.
> — The Best Essay (2024)

> In fact there's an edge case here so spectacular that it turns all the preceding advice on its head. If you want to make a really huge amount of money — hundreds of millions or even billions of dollars — it turns out to be very useful to work on what interests you the most.
> [...]
> So there's something like a midwit peak for making money. If you don't need to make much, you can work on whatever you're most interested in; if you want to become moderately rich, you can't usually afford to; but if you want to become super rich, and you're young and good at technology, working on what you're most interested in becomes a good idea again.
> — When To Do What You Love (2024)

> So yes, the two senses of good writing are connected in at least two ways. [...] But now that we've dissolved one layer of preposterousness, I can't resist adding another. Does sounding good do more than just help you get the ideas right? Is writing that sounds good *inherently* more likely to be right? Crazy as it may seem, I think that's true too.
> — Good Writing (2025)

What to notice: the turns are real, because the essay's conclusion actually changes after them. The "midwit peak" gets a name only once the shape of the idea (low, high, low again) has earned one. And he admits when he started with the wrong question instead of quietly rewriting the opening to hide it.

## 4. Taking the objection seriously

> Ok, many people will be thinking, this seems plausible so far, but what about liars? Is it not notoriously possible for a smooth-tongued liar to write something beautiful that's completely false?
>
> It is, of course. But not without method acting. The way to write something beautiful and false is to begin by making yourself almost believe it. [...] You're saying something that would be true if certain false premises were. If for some bizarre reason the number of jobs in a country were fixed, then immigrants really would be taking our jobs.
>
> So it's not quite right to say that better sounding writing is more likely to be true. Better sounding writing is more likely to be internally consistent. If the writer is honest, internal consistency and truth converge.
> — Good Writing (2025)

What to notice: he states the objection better than a critic would, concedes it ("It is, of course"), and then *narrows his own claim* in response. The essay comes out more precise than it went in. The concrete example (fixed number of jobs) takes one sentence.

## 5. Examples and analogies

He makes a claim and then shows it with the smallest example that works. His analogies usually come from physical things, and he uses one per idea.

> Though no doubt correct, such statements tell the reader nothing. Useful writing makes claims that are as strong as they can be made without becoming false.
>
> For example, it's more useful to say that Pike's Peak is near the middle of Colorado than merely somewhere in Colorado. But if I say it's in the exact middle of Colorado, I've now gone too far, because it's a bit east of the middle.
> — How to Write Usefully (2020)

> The best analogy for this phenomenon is when you shake a bin full of different objects. The shakes are arbitrary motions. Or more precisely, they're not calculated to make any two specific objects fit more closely together. And yet repeated shaking inevitably makes the objects discover brilliantly clever ways of packing themselves. Gravity won't let them become less tightly packed, so any change has to be a change for the better.
>
> So it is with writing. If you have to rewrite an awkward passage, you'll never do it in a way that makes it *less* true.
> — Good Writing (2025)

> For example, Steve Jobs used to run an annual retreat for what he considered the 100 most important people at Apple, and these were not the 100 people highest on the org chart. Can you imagine the force of will it would take to do this at the average company? And yet imagine how useful such a thing could be. It could make a big company feel like a startup. Steve presumably wouldn't have kept having these retreats if they didn't work. But I've never heard of another company doing this. So is it a good idea, or a bad one? We still don't know. That's how little we know about founder mode.
> — Founder Mode (2024)

What to notice: the Pike's Peak example is almost comically small, which is why it works. The bin analogy corrects itself halfway through ("Or more precisely"). The Jobs example ends in open uncertainty, and the uncertainty is the point he's making.

## 6. Naming a thing

> In effect there are two different ways to run a company: founder mode and manager mode. Till now most people even in Silicon Valley have implicitly assumed that scaling a startup meant switching to manager mode. But we can infer the existence of another mode from the dismay of founders who've tried it, and the success of their attempts to escape from it.
> — Founder Mode (2024)

> The other thing you do in the face of uncertainty is to make choices that are uncertainty-proof. The less sure you are about what to do, the more important it is to choose options that give you more options in the future. I call this "staying upwind." If you're unsure whether to major in math or economics, for example, choose math; math is upwind of economics in the sense that it will be easier to switch later from math to economics than from economics to math.
> — When To Do What You Love (2024)

What to notice: the name comes *after* the idea is clear, and it's made of ordinary words. "Staying upwind" gets one sentence of introduction and an example right away. Most of his essays coin nothing at all. One coined term per essay is plenty, and zero is common.

## 7. Precision and hedging

> Resilience means not having one's morale destroyed by setbacks. Setbacks are inevitable once problems reach a certain size, so if you can't bounce back from them, you can only do good work on a small scale. But resilience is not the same as obstinacy. Resilience means setbacks can't change your morale, not that they can't change your mind.
> — The Right Kind of Stubborn (2024)

> When I'm working on an essay, I spend far more time reading than writing. I'll reread some parts 50 or 100 times, replaying the thoughts in them and asking myself, like someone sanding a piece of wood, does anything catch? Does anything feel wrong?
> — Good Writing (2025)

> Obviously founders can't keep running a 2000 person company the way they ran it when it had 20. There's going to have to be some amount of delegation. Where the borders of autonomy end up, and how sharp they are, will probably vary from company to company.
> — Founder Mode (2024)

What to notice: the hedges match what he actually knows ("probably vary", "presumably wouldn't have"). They aren't a verbal tic. The stubbornness passage draws a distinction and then stops. It doesn't dress the distinction up as a revelation.

## 8. Endings

His endings are usually quieter than you'd expect. The most common kind takes the argument one more practical step. Punchlines happen, but they're the exception.

> The discoveries are out there, waiting to be made. Why not by you?
> — How to Do Great Work (2023)

> So if you think you can make something amazing, you should probably just go ahead and do it.
> — What to Do (2025)

> The territory of superlinear returns is by no means static. Indeed, the most extreme returns come from expanding it. So while both ambition and curiosity can get you into this territory, curiosity may be the more powerful of the two. Ambition tends to make you climb existing peaks, but if you stick close enough to an interesting enough question, it may grow into a mountain beneath you.
> — Superlinear Returns (2023)

> Those of us on the maker's schedule are willing to compromise. We know we have to have some number of meetings. All we ask from those on the manager's schedule is that they understand the cost.
> — Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule (2009)

> People's experiences as parents vary a lot, and I know I've been lucky. But I think the worries I had before having kids must be pretty common, and judging by other parents' faces when they see their kids, so must the happiness that kids bring.
> — Having Kids (2019)

> It's hard enough already not to become the prisoner of your own expertise, but it will only get harder, because change is accelerating. That's not a recent trend; change has been accelerating since the paleolithic era. Ideas beget ideas. I don't expect that to change. But I could be wrong.
> — How to Be an Expert in a Changing World (2014)

> Curiously enough it's an encouraging thought that we still know so little about founder mode. Look at what founders have achieved already, and yet they've achieved this against a headwind of bad advice. Imagine what they'll do once we can tell them how to run their companies like Steve Jobs instead of John Sculley.
> — Founder Mode (2024)

What to notice: several of these end on a long sentence. "What to Do" ends on "probably." "Expert in a Changing World" ends by doubting itself. "Maker's Schedule" ends on a modest request. Look at what's missing: none of them summarizes, and none of them repeats the title back as a moral.

## 9. Rhythm at small scale

> I try to write using ordinary words and simple sentences.
>
> That kind of writing is easier to read, and the easier something is to read, the more deeply readers will engage with it. The less energy they expend on your prose, the more they'll have left for your ideas.
>
> [...]
>
> Plus it's more considerate to write simply. When you write in a fancy way to impress people, you're making them do extra work just so you can seem cool. It's like trailing a long train behind you that readers have to carry.
> — Write Simply (2021)

> You don't need complex sentences to express complex ideas. When specialists in some abstruse topic talk to one another about ideas in their field, they don't use sentences any more complex than they do when talking about what to have for lunch. They use different words, certainly. But even those they use no more than necessary. And in my experience, the harder the subject, the more informally experts speak. Partly, I think, because they have less to prove, and partly because the harder the ideas you're talking about, the less you can afford to let language get in the way.
> — Write Like You Talk (2015)

What to notice: "Plus" to start a sentence. "Partly, I think, because..." as a sentence fragment. "Which means..." and "Whereas..." turn up as sentence openers elsewhere. Short sentences carry facts, and long ones carry reasoning with its qualifications attached. Sentence length follows the shape of the thought. It isn't a pattern he's applying.
